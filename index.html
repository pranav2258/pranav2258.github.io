Teaching a Network to Recognize an Attack: A Deep Learning Approach to Intrusion Detection

Notes from my capstone project on using deep learning - specifically recurrent neural networks - to catch DoS and DDoS traffic that traditional intrusion detection systems tend to miss.

Denial of Service and Distributed Denial of Service attacks are some of the oldest tricks in the cyberattack playbook, and also some of the most stubbornly effective. As internet infrastructure has grown more complex, so have these attacks - and traditional signature-based intrusion detection systems (IDS) increasingly struggle to keep up. This project was my attempt to see how much a deep learning approach could close that gap.

The core idea

Rather than relying on static rules to flag known attack signatures, the goal was to build an IDS that could learn the underlying patterns of malicious traffic directly from data - and generalize to variations a rule-based system would miss entirely. I focused specifically on a Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) architecture, since network traffic is inherently sequential: what a packet looks like matters less than what it looks like in context with the packets around it.

Models compared

To validate that the RNN approach was actually worth the added complexity, I benchmarked it against three other deep learning architectures:

  • Deep Belief Network (DBN) - a layered, generative approach to feature learning
  • Stacked Autoencoder - for unsupervised dimensionality reduction on the traffic data
  • Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) - treating traffic features as a spatial pattern
  • LSTM - a gated variant of the RNN family, tested as a direct comparison point

Data & feature selection

Training and evaluation ran on two public benchmark datasets: CSE-CIC-IDS2018, which captures a wide range of normal and malicious traffic scenarios, and CIRA-CIC-DoHBrw-2020, focused specifically on DNS-over-HTTPS traffic. After cleaning and normalizing the raw traffic data, I used a Bayesian information gain model to cut the feature set down to the signals that actually mattered - reducing dimensionality without throwing away the patterns the models needed to learn.

Results

Across accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity, the Deep RNN model came out ahead of the DBN, autoencoder, and CNN baselines, and held its own against LSTM. The gap was most visible in sensitivity - the RNN was better at actually catching attacks rather than just avoiding false alarms, which is the harder and more important half of the problem for a real IDS.

Takeaway: sequence-aware models have a real edge for this problem. Attack traffic isn't just anomalous in isolation - it's anomalous in sequence, and architectures built to exploit that structure outperform ones that treat each packet independently.

What's next

This is still an active area I want to keep pushing on - fine-tuning hyperparameters further, testing additional architectures, and expanding beyond DoS/DDoS to a broader range of attack types so the model stays useful against threats that keep evolving. The paper is currently unpublished; I'll update this post once it's out.
